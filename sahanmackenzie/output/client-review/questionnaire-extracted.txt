P0153 08   Origins
P0154 When did spaces first catch your attention? What shaped your taste growing up: family, particular homes, places, fashion, art?
P0155 When did spaces first catch your attention?
P0156 My fascination with interiors began long before I understood interior design as a profession. I was naturally drawn to the way a room could change simply through proportion, arrangement, colour, texture, light, and the objects within it.
P0157 Even as a child, I found myself looking at rooms differently. I would imagine how a space could be rearranged, how a particular piece of furniture might sit differently, or how changing a colour, fabric, or accessory could completely alter the character of a room. Spaces were never simply rooms to me; they were compositions that could continually be reconsidered.
P0158 I was particularly intrigued by rooms that were not simple rectangles—spaces with unusual proportions, architectural details, corners, transitions, or limitations. Rather than seeing these as difficulties, I found myself wondering how they could become part of the design.
P0159 What shaped your taste growing up?
P0160 My eye developed through an accumulation of visual experiences rather than from one single influence. Homes, furnishings, fabrics, decorative objects, architecture, fashion, art, and the way people lived within their spaces all contributed to how I began to understand aesthetics.
P0161 I was especially attracted to design catalogues and the visual language of interiors. I could spend time studying how different materials, colours, furniture forms and decorative elements were brought together to create a particular atmosphere.
P0162 Over time, I became increasingly aware that good design is rarely about one beautiful object. It is about the relationship between everything—the architecture, furniture, lighting, materials, textures, colours and the people who ultimately inhabit the space.
P0163 Was there a moment when you realised this was more than an interest?
P0164 My curiosity gradually became a desire to understand the discipline behind what I was instinctively noticing. Design & Architecture programmes, design catalogues and exposure to different styles gave structure to an interest that had already been developing naturally.
P0165 I began to understand that what I had always found fascinating about interiors was not simply their appearance, but the thinking behind them: why something belongs in a particular place, how proportion creates harmony, how materials influence atmosphere, and how a space can respond to the personality and needs of the person living in it.
P0166 What did you naturally do before formal design training?
P0167 I experimented.
P0168 I would imagine alternative ways of arranging rooms, reconsider furniture placement, play with colours and soft furnishings, and mentally redesign spaces around me. I was interested in discovering what could happen when familiar elements were given a different relationship with one another.
P0169 That instinct—to question, refine and reconsider—is still central to the way I approach design today.
P0170 What qualities from your early life remain in your work?
P0171 Curiosity remains one of the strongest.
P0172 I have always believed that there is more to discover within a space than what is immediately visible. A room may have beautiful architecture, but its true character emerges through the details: the texture of a fabric, the scale of a piece of furniture, the warmth of a material, the quality of light, the placement of an object, or even the way one space leads into another.
P0173 This is perhaps where my interest in design minutiae originates—the belief that seemingly small decisions can collectively create an extraordinary whole.
P0174 For me, interior design has never been simply about making a space beautiful. It is about observing, understanding, editing and composing until practicality and beauty converge, and the resulting interior feels considered, personal and complete.

P0183 Career Turning Points

P0185 AOD / Northumbria — Learning to Think as a Designer

P0187 I learned to think about space more critically—to understand proportion, functionality, materials, presentation and the relationship between an idea and its execution. P0188 Aviraté / Timex — Understanding Design Through Brand, Detail & the Consumer

P0192 Clove Republic — Translating Design Into Real Spaces

P0196 It was also here that I learned to balance creative ambition with practicality, budget, functionality, craftsmanship and execution—the realities that ultimately determine whether a beautiful concept becomes a successful interior.

P0199 Education taught me how to think.

P0205 My design identity therefore became less about following a particular style and more about developing a considered way of seeing.

P0237 11   Decisions and disagreement
P0238 When a big decision comes up, what do you rely on: logic, intuition or experience? Describe a time you strongly disagreed with a client or collaborator, and how you handled it.
P0239 When a big decision comes up, what do you rely on: logic, intuition or experience?
P0240 I rely on all three, but I have learned that each has its place.
P0241 Experience helps me recognise what is practical, what is likely to work, and where potential problems may arise before they become problems.
P0242 Logic helps me evaluate the realities of a decision proportion, functionality, materials, budget, maintenance, execution and how the individual elements will work together.
P0243 Intuition is what allows the design to become something more than a technically correct solution. It is often the instinct that tells me that a particular colour, material, object or composition belongs—even before I can fully articulate why.
P0244 The best design decisions usually happen when these three meet.
P0245 I don't believe in designing purely from intuition, because a beautiful idea still needs to function. Equally, I don't believe design should become so logical that it loses character. My approach is to allow intuition to propose, experience to question, and logic to refine.
P0246 A time I strongly disagreed with a client or collaborator
P0247 There have been occasions when a client's initial preference and my professional recommendation have been quite different.
P0248 My approach in those situations is not to simply insist that my idea is right. Instead, I try to understand why the client is drawn to their preferred direction. Sometimes the disagreement reveals something important about what they actually want from the space.
P0249 I explain my reasoning through the design itself—using proportions, material samples, colour relationships, visual references, drawings, elevations or alternatives where appropriate. Rather than saying “this won't work,” I prefer to demonstrate why one solution may create a stronger result and what the consequences of another choice might be.
P0250 There have also been situations where the client's perspective has caused me to reconsider my own initial position. I see that as part of the design process rather than a loss of creative control.
P0251 Ultimately, I believe the designer's role is not to win an argument. It is to guide the client towards the strongest possible outcome while ensuring that the finished space still feels like theirs.
P0252 A successful collaboration is not one where everyone agrees immediately. It is one where different perspectives can be challenged, understood and ultimately translated into a better design.

P0257 12   Changed views
P0258 What do you believe now that you didn’t believe when you started out?
P0259 When I first began, I believed good interior design was primarily about creating something beautiful.
P0260 I still believe beauty matters—but over the years, I have come to understand that beauty alone is not enough.
P0261 I now believe that the most successful interiors are not necessarily the ones with the most impressive furniture, the most expensive materials, or the most elaborate decoration. They are the spaces where every decision has a reason and every element has a relationship with something else.
P0262 Experience has taught me to look beyond the immediate visual impact of a room and ask deeper questions:
P0263 How will this space actually be lived in?
P0264 Does it reflect the person who inhabits it?
P0265 Will the materials age gracefully?
P0266 Does the furniture work with the architecture?
P0267 Is there balance between what is necessary and what is simply beautiful?
P0268 Does the space still have something to say years from now?
P0269 I have also changed my view of constraints. Earlier in my career, limitations could feel like obstacles to a design. Today, I often see them as opportunities for creativity. A difficult proportion, an existing piece of furniture, a restricted budget, an unusual architectural condition or a client's particular preference can become the starting point for a more thoughtful solution.
P0270 Perhaps the greatest change is that I no longer believe that a designer's success is measured by how much of themselves they can put into a space.
P0271 It is measured by how well they can understand someone else and translate that understanding into an environment that feels authentic to them.
P0272 That has made me more selective, more observant and more willing to edit.
P0273 I have come to believe that refinement is often less about adding more and more about knowing what deserves to remain.
P0274 And perhaps most importantly, I have learned that timelessness is not the absence of personality. A timeless interior can be expressive, distinctive and deeply personal—it simply needs to be grounded in something more enduring than a passing trend.
P0275 Today, I see interior design less as the act of decorating a space and more as the art of composing a life within it.
P0276 The final sentence has strong potential for my eventual brand language:
P0277 “Interior design is the art of composing a life within a space.”
P0278 It also connects naturally with my existing “The Art of Timeless Living” positioning without simply repeating it.

P1256 33   Where money leaks
P1257 On which projects did you lose money or time, and why: revisions, scope creep, late payments, suppliers? What payment structure do you use now?
P1258 The areas where projects can lose the most time and profitability are usually not the creative work itself, but uncontrolled changes around the creative work.
P1259 The main areas I have identified are:
P1260 1. Scope creepA project can gradually expand beyond the original brief—additional rooms, furniture, styling, sourcing, drawings, site involvement, or revisions—without the commercial scope being adjusted accordingly.
P1261 2. Revisions beyond the agreed scopeDesign is naturally collaborative, and I welcome considered client feedback. However, repeated revisions or changes after a design direction has already been approved can consume significant creative and production time.
P1262 The future system should distinguish between normal design refinement and a genuine change in scope.
P1263 3. Late decisions and approvalsWhen decisions are delayed, the effect is not limited to one decision. It can affect suppliers, manufacturing, procurement, site progress, installation dates, and ultimately the overall programme.
P1264 4. Supplier and procurement delaysCustom furniture, imported materials, specialist finishes, and bespoke elements can introduce variables that are outside the designer's direct control. If these are not tracked carefully, time can be lost through follow-ups, substitutions, delivery changes, or incorrect information.
P1265 5. Unplanned site involvementAdditional site visits, repeated coordination, or resolving issues that were not included in the original scope can gradually increase the amount of professional time invested in a project.
P1266 6. Payment delaysWhen payment milestones are not aligned with the project stages, the atelier can end up carrying significant amounts of work, procurement commitments, or supplier payments before receiving the corresponding client payment.
P1267 What I Have Learned
P1268 The biggest lesson is that good design needs good commercial boundaries.
P1269 I do not want stronger commercial systems to make the client experience feel rigid. I want them to create clarity so that the creative relationship remains positive.
P1270 The client should know:
P1271 What is included
P1272 What is not included
P1273 How many design stages are covered
P1274 How revisions are handled
P1275 When approvals are required
P1276 When additional work becomes a variation
P1277 What procurement responsibilities are included
P1278 What site involvement is included
P1279 When payments are due
P1280 What happens when the programme changes because of delayed decisions
P1281 Payment Structure
P1282 The exact payment structure for Mackenzie's Atelier should be formalised rather than relying on one arrangement for every project.
P1283 My preferred future approach is to connect payments to clear project milestones, for example:
P1284 Appointment / Initial Deposit → Concept & Design Development → Detailed Design / Documentation → Procurement / Implementation → Final Stage / Handover
P1285 The exact percentage or amount at each stage should be determined based on the project scope, procurement requirements, and level of involvement.
P1286 For projects involving significant bespoke furniture, imported materials, or procurement commitments, client funds should be received before the atelier is required to make corresponding supplier or manufacturing commitments, rather than allowing the atelier to finance the project.
P1287 Future Commercial Protection
P1288 I would like every project to have a clear mechanism for handling additional work:
P1289 Original Scope → Approved Change → Additional Fee / Cost → Client Approval → Proceed
P1290 This means a client can absolutely change their mind or expand the project, but the commercial implications are made visible before additional work begins.
P1291 I also want project documentation to make approvals and decisions traceable. This connects directly to the operating principles of the atelier: clarity protects creativity.
P1292 The Bigger Lesson
P1293 I do not want to become overly focused on protecting every minute or turning the design process into a rigid transaction.
P1294 Instead, I want Mackenzie's Atelier to become better at recognising where professional time, creative energy, procurement responsibility, and financial exposure are increasing—and to address those changes early.
P1295 The objective is not to charge for every conversation. It is to ensure that when the scope, responsibility, or workload genuinely changes, the commercial structure changes with it.
P1296 That allows me to remain generous with the client relationship while protecting the time and attention required to maintain the standard of Design Minutiae.
